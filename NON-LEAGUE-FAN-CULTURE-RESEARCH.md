## Groundhopper Voices — Fresh Interviews

### Matt the Groundhopper — "Why I've Fallen for Non-League Grounds" (Groundhopper Weekly, Sep 2025)

Matt is completing a journey to visit all 24 National League South grounds. Three quarters of the way through, he shares what keeps him going:

> *"Over the last couple of years, I've found myself enjoying non-league football grounds more than the bigger Football League stadiums. There's something about the friendlier approach, the more affordable prices, and the sheer variety of grounds that really appeals to me. Many of these places haven't been upgraded in years. Rather than being a drawback, it actually gives them character — a throwback feel that takes me back to watching old footage from the 1970s and 80s. You don't just watch football here; you experience a slice of history."*
> — **Matt the Groundhopper, Groundhopper Weekly**

Groundhopper Weekly readers responded with equal warmth:

> *"We've been loving visiting non-league grounds. There's so much more character and personality; each one feels slightly different. We also love seeing what food's on offer — usually much better than even Premier League stadiums!"*
> — **Sarah, Groundhopper Weekly Substack comments**

> *"Extremely well written and presented piece that showcases both the highs and lows of non-league grounds. I too share many of the same thoughts, especially the freedom and sense of community elements that you just don't get in the pro game anymore."*
> — **The Football Pyramid, Substack**

Matt's "Three Grounds That Miss the Mark" observation is also notable for what it reveals about ground design and atmosphere:

> *"Chelmsford City feels oddly temporary... Enfield Town is the most charming thanks to its art deco main stand... Hornchurch — my least favourite. The main stand is unremarkable, the open terracing is sparse... It felt flat, distant, and honestly one of my worst non-league experiences so far."*
> — **Matt the Groundhopper**

The key insight from Matt's journey — non-league football's greatest strength is its variety:

> *"I wouldn't trade my National League South journey for anything. The passion, the variety, the history — it's what makes ground-hopping so addictive. Crowds may be smaller than in the professional game, but the atmosphere can be electric, especially when big ex-League clubs bring large travelling supports. It's real football, lived and felt at close quarters."*
> — **Matt the Groundhopper**

### Paul Gerald & Groundhopper Guides — "The Authenticity of Groundhopping" (Game Day Stories, Jan 2026)

Paul Gerald became addicted to groundhopping after spotting Stockport County's Edgeley Park from Manchester Piccadilly train window. His most profound encounter came years later:

> *"Years later, I went there and found myself sitting next to a man well into his 80s. We struck up a conversation, and I asked how long he'd been coming to games here. 'Since me dad got back from the war,' he said. His first game was in like 1948. I asked if he remembers where he sat that night, and pointed across the way and said, 'On that stand right here, only back then it weren't a stand, just a terrace.'"*
> — **Paul Gerald, founder of Groundhopper Guides — Game Day Stories**

Matt Coughlan, author of *Turncoat* and *Groundhopper: Tales from Football's Equivalent of Trainspotting* (2025), visited around 140 grounds before writing his book:

> *"I don't feel like I've ever felt like I've wasted a journey anywhere, and there are a lot of highlights where I've gone to a lot of random places that you wouldn't think about, like Redbridge, Biggleswade, places you wouldn't go on holiday, but are quite good to explore through the medium of football."*
> — **Matt Coughlan, Game Day Stories**

> *"(A groundhopper) is someone who's willing to go to a random game where you don't support one of the teams. You start going to games and you keep going."*
> — **Matt Coughlan**

Alex Irons runs Glory Hunter Football and documents non-league culture on YouTube and TikTok:

> *"I can't watch Fulham these days because I'm often outpriced... I've relied on non-league football more and more. I've always known about the smaller teams where I grew up. My dad made an effort to take us to watch clubs if they were on at home. We'd go and watch Wembley, North Greenford... And it was quite nice to do that because you go and just see how other clubs do things, meet new people."*
> — **Alex Irons, Glory Hunter — Game Day Stories**

> *"I think that anyone who just enjoys football is inherently someone who you could classify as a groundhopper. Someone who just loves the culture and loves being around football, it doesn't matter who's playing, where it is, as long as someone is there."*
> — **Alex Irons**

> *"People increasingly like the accessibility, the community, the cost and all the convenience you can find from Saturday 3 p.m. fixtures. There's always something to go to whenever you're free to do it and it fits in nicely rather than a schedule dictated by Sky."*
> — **Matt Coughlan**

### Soccer Wizdom — "Wanderers of the Stands" (Apr 2026)

A sweeping taxonomy of groundhopper types that has become widely shared across supporter communities:

#### The Four Groundhopper Archetypes

| Archetype | Motivation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The Collector** | Tick off as many stadiums as possible | Entering a new era of groundhoppers and app-based tracking |
| **The Historian** | Seek out heritage grounds, abandoned sites, stories | Visiting the oldest grounds in football's history |
| **The Adventurer** | Chase unusual experiences — remote pitches, extreme weather | Willing to travel internationally to lesser-known grounds |
| **The Social Hopper** | Joy comes from meeting fans across borders | Making friends from different countries on away days |

> *"Groundhopping often takes supporters to smaller clubs where the game feels more intimate. The connection between fans and players is closer. The atmosphere is unfiltered."*
> — **Soccer Wizdom, Wanderers of the Stands**

Key rituals that define the modern groundhopper:

> *"Groundhoppers often arrive early. They walk around the stadium. They explore nearby pubs. They talk to locals. They absorb the environment long before kickoff."*
> — **Soccer Wizdom**

## The Away Day Tradition — Expanded

### The 5 Components of the Away Day Experience

Based on FSA criteria, Football Ground Guide research, and fan discussions:

1. **The Welcome** — Volunteers greet away fans at the gate, point to facilities, offer directions. Does anyone know why the following matters today?
2. **The Pub Before** — Away fans gather at a designated pub (often chosen by the home club). Some clubs arrange away-train minibus services — the Northern "shuttle bus" tradition.
3. **The Match** — Limited segregation at non-league level; opposition fans often mingle freely in terraces; chants and songs cross the divide.
4. **The Food** — Local bakeries provide pies and pasties; clubs like Farnham Town have won praise for food quality (£7.50 sweet chilli fried chicken and rice).
5. **The Pub After** — Opposing fans leave together; conversation continues; friendships form.

## Non-League Day — 15th Anniversary

### History

Non-League Day was founded in **2011** by James Dowd and a group of supporters who wanted to celebrate the non-league game. The first Non-League Day took place on **Saturday 27 August 2011**, with thousands of fans across the country attending their local non-league club for free or reduced admission.

The event has grown year-on-year and is now the **biggest celebration of the non-league game in the calendar**.

### 2026 — 15th Anniversary

- **Date:** Saturday 28 March 2026
- **Charity partner:** Prostate Cancer UK (partnership since 2014)
- **Scope:** Hundreds of clubs across the National League and wider non-league pyramid open their gates to the public
- **The investment:** The Premier League has committed £23.6M in solidarity payments to National League clubs — coinciding with the anniversary year

> Non-League Day has raised over £500,000 for Prostate Cancer UK since 2014, making it one of the most impactful supporter-led charity campaigns in British sport.

*Source: nonleagueday.com; Football Supporters' Association*

## The Match Day Food Culture

### The "Footy Scran" Tradition

Non-league food has become a major differentiator. Surveys of football fans consistently identify food quality as one of the top 3 factors when rating a ground.

| Food | Typical Price | Where to Find |
|---|---|---|
| Meat & potato pie + mash + gravy | £3–4 | Most NL clubs; local bakery partners |
| Gourmet burger | £3.50–5 | Larger NL clubs and more progressive town-centre grounds |
| Pasty | £3–4 | Cornish clubs including Falmouth Town |
| Fried chicken + chips | £4–5 | Farnham Town (voted one of the best in non-league) |
| Curry | £4–5 | Various clubs with Indian sub-continent communities |
| Craft beer | £3–4 | Lewes FC, Falmouth Town, and others |
| Programme | £2–3 | Every paper programme in the league |

> *"Forget the overpriced, lukewarm hotdogs found in the top flight. In the lower leagues, the food is often the star of the show. Many clubs have embraced the 'Footy Scran' revolution, partnering with local bakeries to serve legendary steak and ale pies."*
> — **The Non-League Football Paper, Feb 2026**

## Regional Culture — Deep Dive

### The North
- **Shuttle bus tradition** — fans share minibuses to away games, singing all the way
- FC Halifax Town's 14,000-capacity ground gives a Football League feel
- **Leeds, Bradford, Huddersfield, Halifax** — some of the fiercest but friendliest rivalries
- Industrial heritage still visible at many grounds (Odsal, Throstle Nest, Moor Lane)

### The Midlands
- Strong working-class roots with post-match socialising traditions
- Derbies rooted in actual geography — Birmingham derbies span just 6 miles but carry generational weight
- Home to some of the oldest continuous football communities (e.g. Cambridge United, Coventry Sphinx

### South / South West
- Coastal and holiday-town atmospheres — Torquay, Falmouth, Lewes, Worthing
- Gentrification influences from London overrunning into the_HOME Counties
- Growing number of fan-owned and community clubs
- New British-style smaller grounds (Poole, Salisbury, Cirencester)

### London
- Giant-killing culture — Leyton Orient's 120-year history, Dagenham & Redbridge's FA Cup giant-killings
- Diverse communities — clubs reflect their immediate neighbourhoods;estopnie's Brimsdown, Lewisham, etc.

### Scotland
- SPFL integration — Scottish non-league clubs operate within the broader SPFL ecosystem
- Distinctive terrace traditions — the "flask" culture, pre-match Irish-Scots songs, and the Scottish ground着自己的传统 (songs, rivalry with clubs like Alloa, Stirling Albion)
